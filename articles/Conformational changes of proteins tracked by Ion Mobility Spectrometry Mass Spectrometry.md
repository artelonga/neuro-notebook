The function of proteins is strongly linked to their structure, which arises from the folding of a polypeptide chain made up of 20 canonical amino acids. This sequence determines the formation of secondary structures, such as α-helices and β-sheets, and the overall tertiary structure. In some cases, proteins form quaternary structures through defined intermolecular interactions. Mutations can disrupt these interactions, impacting protein flexibility, folding, and function. Native mass spectrometry (MS) and ion mobility spectrometry (IMS) enable detailed analysis of protein structures, including subunit composition, mutation detection, and conformational states. Techniques like collision-induced unfolding (CIU) allow the study of structural stability and conformational changes. This dissertation explored protein folding and dynamics using these methods. A key focus was amyloid-β42 (Aβ42), a peptide associated with Alzheimer’s disease. Studies revealed that Aβ42 forms early oligomers in two distinct conformations, with aggregation resembling a zipper model from the tetramer stage onward. The aggregation inhibitor CLR01 was shown to block zipper-like dimer formation, resulting in amorphous, likely non-toxic aggregates. Mass spectrometry confirmed that CLR01 slowed oligomer growth, supporting its potential therapeutic relevance. To further refine the methodology, the photoactive yellow protein (PYP) was used as a model to develop time-resolved IMS-MS for tracking light-induced conformational changes. These experiments demonstrated that photoactivation causes unfolding and exposure of the N-terminus, as seen through increased charge states and collision cross-sections. The approach was then applied to the light-sensitive cryptochrome CraCRY from Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, revealing conformational differences between its ground and activated states and enabling analysis of reaction kinetics. Overall, the work highlights the value of native MS and IMS-MS in characterizing the effects of intra- and intermolecular interactions on protein structure and dynamics. The integration of experimental and simulated data, combined with the ability to extract kinetic information, represents a methodological advance with applications in drug discovery, optogenetics, and the study of flexible or transient protein states.
