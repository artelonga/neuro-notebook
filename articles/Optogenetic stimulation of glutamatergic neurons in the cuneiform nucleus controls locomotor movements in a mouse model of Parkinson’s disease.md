ABSTRACT In Parkinson’s disease ( PD ), the loss of midbrain [[grafo/termos/dopaminergic cells|dopaminergic cells]] results in severe [[grafo/termos/locomotor deficits|locomotor deficits]] such a gait freezing and [[grafo/termos/akinesia|akinesia]]. Growing [[grafo/termos/evidence indicates|evidence indicates]] that these deficits can be attributed to decreased activity in the [[grafo/termos/mesencephalic locomotor region|Mesencephalic Locomotor Region]] ( MLR ), a [[grafo/termos/brainstem|brainstem]] region controlling [[grafo/termos/locomotion|locomotion]]. Clinicians are exploring [[grafo/termos/deep brain stimulation|deep brain stimulation]] of the MLR as a [[grafo/termos/treatment options|treatment option]] to improve locomotor function. The results are variable, from modest to promising. However, within the MLR, clinicians have targeted the [[grafo/termos/pedunculopontine nucleus|pedunculopontine nucleus]] exclusively, while leaving the [[grafo/termos/cuneiform nucleus|cuneiform nucleus]] unexplored. To our knowledge, the effects of cuneiform nucleus stimulation have never been determined in [[grafo/termos/parkinsonian conditions|parkinsonian conditions]] in any animal model. Here, we addressed this issue in a mouse model of Parkinson’s disease based on bilateral striatal injection of [[grafo/termos/6-hydroxydopamine|6-hydroxydopamine]] ( 6-OHDA ), which damaged the [[grafo/termos/nigrostriatal pathway|nigrostriatal pathway]] and decreased [[grafo/termos/locomotor activity|locomotor activity]]. We show that selective optogenetic stimulation of [[grafo/termos/glutamatergic neurons|glutamatergic neurons]] in the cuneiform nucleus in mice expressing [[grafo/termos/channelrhodopsin|channelrhodopsin]] in a Cre-dependent manner in Vglut2-positive neurons ( Vglut2-ChR2-EYFP mice ) increased the number of locomotor initiations, increased the time spent in locomotion, and controlled locomotor speed. Using deep learning-based movement analysis, we found that limb kinematics of optogenetic-evoked locomotion in pathological conditions were largely similar to those recorded in [[grafo/termos/freely moving|freely moving]] animals. Our work identifies the glutamatergic neurons of the cuneiform nucleus as a potentially clinically relevant target to improve locomotor activity in parkinsonian conditions. Our study should open new avenues to develop targeted stimulation of these neurons using deep brain stimulation, pharmacotherapy or optogenetics. SIGNIFICANCE STATEMENT In Parkinson’s disease, alleviating locomotor deficits is a challenge. Clinicians are exploring deep brain stimulation of the Mesencephalic Locomotor Region, a brainstem region controlling locomotion, but results are mixed. However, the best target in this region in Parkinson’s disease remains unknown. Indeed, this region which comprises the pedunculopontine and cuneiform nuclei, contains different [[grafo/termos/cell types|cell types]] with opposing effects on locomotor output. Here, using a mouse model where midbrain dopaminergic cells were damaged by a neurotoxin, we demonstrate that optogenetic activation of glutamatergic neurons in the cuneiform nucleus increases locomotion, controls speed, and evokes limb movements similar to those observed during spontaneous locomotion in intact animals. Our study identifies a potentially clinically relevant target to improve locomotor function in Parkinson’s disease.
