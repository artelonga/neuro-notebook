The [[grafo/termos/microtubule-associated protein tau|microtubule-associated protein tau]] aggregates into oligomeric complexes that highly correlate with Alzheimer's disease (AD) progression. Increasing evidence suggests that nuclear membrane disruption occurs in AD and related tauopathies, but whether this is a cause or consequence of [[grafo/termos/neurodegeneration|neurodegeneration]] [[grafo/termos/remains unclear|remains unclear]]. Using the optogenetically inducible 4R1N [[grafo/termos/tau|Tau]]::mCherry::Cry2Olig (optoTau) system in iPSC-derived neurons, we demonstrate that [[grafo/termos/tau oligomerization|tau oligomerization]] triggers nuclear rupture and nuclear membrane invagination. [[grafo/termos/pathological tau|Pathological tau]] accumulates at sites of invagination, inducing structural abnormalities in the nuclear envelope and piercing into the nuclear space. These findings were confirmed in the humanized [[grafo/termos/p301s tau|P301S tau]] (PS19) transgenic mouse model, where nuclear envelope disruption appeared as an early-onset event preceding neurodegeneration. Further validation in post-mortem AD [[grafo/termos/brain tissue|brain tissues]] revealed nuclear lamina disruption correlating with pathological tau emergence in [[grafo/termos/early stages|early-stage]] patients. Notably, electron microscopy shows that tau-induced nuclear invagination triggers global chromatin reorganization, potentially driving aberrant [[grafo/termos/gene expression|gene expression]] and protein translation associated with AD. These findings suggest that nuclear membrane disruption is an early and possibly causative event in tau-mediated neurodegeneration, establishing a mechanistic link between tau oligomerization and nuclear stress. Further investigation into nuclear destabilization could inform clinical strategies for mitigating AD pathogenesis.
