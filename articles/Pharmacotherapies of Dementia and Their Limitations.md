Dementia refers to the loss of [[grafo/termos/cognitive function|cognitive function]] and social skills significant enough to affect an individual&s;s daily life. It affects 5–8% of individuals 65 years old, and its incidence doubles every five years thereafter. About 82 and 152 million people worldwide are projected to suffer from dementia by 2030 and 2050, respectively. Dementia could be progressive and irreversible or reversible. The commonest type, however, is Alzheimer&s;s disease dementia. The [[grafo/termos/pathophysiological mechanisms|pathophysiological mechanisms]] that underlie dementia include extracellular [[grafo/termos/amyloid beta|amyloid beta]] deposition, intracellular [[grafo/termos/neurofibrillary tangles|neurofibrillary tangle]] formation, [[grafo/termos/neuroinflammation|neuroinflammation]], and [[grafo/termos/oxidative stress|oxidative stress]]. While nonpharmacological approaches such as optogenetics have been proposed, pharmacotherapy remains the mainstay of treatment. The major drugs in clinical use are cholinesterase inhibitors and N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) [[grafo/termos/receptor antagonist|receptor antagonist]] – memantine, which give symptomatic relief but do not alter disease progression nor offer [[grafo/termos/neuroprotection|neuroprotection]]. Several drugs are used as adjuncts, while others that have been hypothesised to alter the disease progression have not passed [[grafo/termos/clinical trials|clinical trials]]. Hence, there is a need for further research targeting a personalised approach to the management of dementia.
