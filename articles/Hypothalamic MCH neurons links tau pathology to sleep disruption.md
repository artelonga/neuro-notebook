[[grafo/termos/sleep|Sleep]] disruption is an early and pervasive feature of Alzheimer's disease (AD), yet the [[grafo/termos/circuit mechanisms|circuit mechanisms]] linking [[grafo/termos/tau pathology|tau pathology]] to sleep-wake dysfunction remain unresolved. Here, we identify melanin-concentrating hormone (MCH) neurons in the lateral hypothalamus (LH) as a critical node disrupted in [[grafo/termos/tauopathy|tauopathy]]. Longitudinal [[grafo/termos/eeg|EEG]]/EMG recordings in PS19 mice reveal progressive impairments in sleep architecture and homeostasis. Histological analyses revealed significant degeneration of both MCH neurons and the neighboring hypocretin (Hcrt) [[grafo/termos/neuronal populations|neuronal population]] in the LH at late stages of tauopathy. In vivo [[grafo/termos/fiber photometry|fiber photometry]] recordings demonstrated a selective [[grafo/termos/functional impairments|functional impairment]] of MCH neurons, characterized by reduced activity during [[grafo/termos/rem sleep|REM sleep]], whereas Hcrt [[grafo/termos/neuronal activity|neuronal activity]] remained largely preserved. In addition, cell-autonomous expression of mutant [[grafo/termos/tau|tau]] in MCH neurons recapitulates sleep disruption, establishing a causal role. Despite tauopathy-induced [[grafo/termos/neuronal loss|neuronal loss]] and reduced endogenous activity, optogenetic and [[grafo/termos/chemogenetic activation|chemogenetic activation]] show that MCH neurons retain functional capacity, and their activation restores sleep in aged PS19 mice. Together, these findings define a circuit mechanism linking tau pathology to sleep and identify MCH neurons as a tractable [[grafo/termos/therapeutic target|therapeutic target]].
