Recent breakthroughs in optogenetic technologies to alter [[grafo/termos/neuronal firing|neuronal firing]] and function with light, combined with [[grafo/termos/cell-type specific|cell type-specific]] transgenic animal lines, has led to important insights into the function of distinct [[grafo/termos/neuronal cell|neuronal cell]] subtypes and afferent connections in the heterogeneously complex [[grafo/termos/striatum|striatum]]. A vital part of the [[grafo/termos/basal ganglia|basal ganglia]], the striatum is heavily implicated in both [[grafo/termos/motor control|motor control]] and motivation-based behavior; as well as in [[grafo/termos/neurological disorders|neurological disorders]] and psychiatric diseases including Parkinson's Disease, Huntington's Disease, [[grafo/termos/drug addiction|drug addiction]], [[grafo/termos/depression|depression]], and schizophrenia. Researchers are able to manipulate firing and cell signaling with temporal precision using optogenetics in the two [[grafo/termos/striatal medium spiny neurons|striatal medium spiny neuron]] (MSN) subpopulations, the striatal interneurons, and striatal afferents. These studies confirmed the classical hypothesis of movement control and [[grafo/termos/reward seeking|reward seeking]] behavior through direct versus [[grafo/termos/indirect pathway|indirect pathway]] [[grafo/termos/medium spiny neurons|MSNs]]; illuminated a selective role for TANs in cocaine [[grafo/termos/reward|reward]]; dissected the roles of glutamatergic and dopaminergic inputs to striatum in reward; and highlighted a role for striatal signaling molecules including an adrenergic G-protein coupled receptor in reward and the rho-GTPase Rac1 in cocaine reward and cocaine induced structural plasticity. This review focuses on how the evolving optogenetic toolbox provides insight into the distinct behavioral roles of striatal cell subpopulations and striatal afferents, which has clinically relevant implications into neurological disorders and psychiatric disease.
