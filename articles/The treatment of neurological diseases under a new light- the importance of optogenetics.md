Controlling activity of defined populations of neurons without affecting other neurons in the brain is now possible by a new gene- and neuroengineering technology termed optogenetics. Derived from microbial organisms, opsin genes encoding light-activated [[grafo/termos/ion channels|ion channels]] and pumps ([[grafo/termos/channelrhodopsin-2|channelrhodopsin-2]] [ChR2]; [[grafo/termos/halorhodopsin|halorhodopsin]] [NpHR], respectively), engineered for expression in the [[grafo/termos/mammalian brain|mammalian brain]], can be [[grafo/termos/genetically targeted|genetically targeted]] into specific [[grafo/termos/neural population|neural populations]] using [[grafo/termos/viral vectors|viral vectors]]. When exposed to light with appropriate wavelength, [[grafo/termos/action potentials|action potentials]] can be triggered in ChR2-expressing neurons, whereas inhibition of action potentials can be obtained in NpHR-expressing neurons, thus allowing for powerful control of [[grafo/termos/neural activity|neural activity]]. Optogenetics is now intensively used in laboratory animals, both in vitro and in vivo, for exploring functions of complex [[grafo/termos/neural circuits|neural circuits]] and [[grafo/termos/information processing|information processing]] in the normal brain and during various [[grafo/termos/neurological conditions|neurological conditions]]. The clinical perspectives of adopting optogenetics as a novel treatment strategy for human [[grafo/termos/neurological disorders|neurological disorders]] have generated considerable interest, largely because of the enormous potential demonstrated in recent rodent and [[grafo/termos/nonhuman primates|nonhuman primate]] studies. Restoration of dopamine-related movement dysfunction in [[grafo/termos/parkinsonian animals|parkinsonian animals]], amelioration of blindness and recovery of breathing after [[grafo/termos/spinal cord injury|spinal cord injury]] are a few examples of such perspectives.
