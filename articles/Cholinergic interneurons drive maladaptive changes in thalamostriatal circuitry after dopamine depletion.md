[[grafo/termos/direct and indirect pathways|Direct and indirect pathway]] [[grafo/termos/spiny projection neurons|spiny projection neurons]] in the [[grafo/termos/striatum|striatum]] bidirectionally modulate the output nuclei of the [[grafo/termos/basal ganglia|basal ganglia]]. An imbalance of these 2 pathways is a key contributor to the [[grafo/termos/motor symptoms|motor symptoms]] of Parkinson’s disease (PD). In the classical model of striatal function, [[grafo/termos/dopamine|dopamine]] promotes movement by activating stimulatory D1 [[grafo/termos/dopamine receptors|dopamine receptors]] on “direct” striatonigral spiny projection neurons (dSPNs) and inhibitory D2 receptors on “indirect” striatopallidal SPNs (iSPNs). In this model, dopamine loss tips the scales toward the movement-suppressing [[grafo/termos/indirect pathway|indirect pathway]], resulting in hypokinetic PD symptoms. SPNs are synaptically connected to many [[grafo/termos/cell types|cell types]]. Beyond the direct impacts of dopamine loss on SPN function, dopamine loss initiates a cascade of circuit adaptations that can indirectly disrupt SPN function. These maladaptations are thought to be a significant cause of [[grafo/termos/motor dysfunction|motor dysfunction]] in PD but are [[grafo/termos/poorly understood|poorly understood]]. A new study by Tanimura and colleagues advances understanding of circuit adaptations in PD, reporting a novel and behaviorally relevant adaptation mediated by parafascicular thalamostriatal [[grafo/termos/projection neurons|projection neurons]] (PFn) and [[grafo/termos/striatal cholinergic interneurons|striatal cholinergic interneurons]] (ChI). Using a combination of pharmacologic, [[grafo/termos/chemogenetics|chemogenetic]], and optogenetic techniques, they demonstrate that in the [[grafo/termos/6-hydroxydopamine|6-hydroxydopamine]] (6-OHDA) PD model, PFn exhibit heightened connectivity with iSPNs, thereby enhancing iSPN activity. Interestingly, this effect requires ChI activity, as chemogenetic suppression of these cells significantly reduced the enhanced PFn-iSPN connectivity. Additional studies demonstrate that in 6-OHDA mice, ChIs potentiate PFn terminals that [[grafo/termos/synapse|synapse]] on iSPNs via increased [[grafo/termos/acetylcholine|acetylcholine]] release acting upon α6-containing [[grafo/termos/nicotinic acetylcholine receptors|nicotinic acetylcholine receptors]] (nAChRs). Two causes were identified for this increased ACh release. One is the well-known loss of D2 autoreceptor-mediated ChI inhibition. Second, Tanimura and colleagues identified a novel source of ChI hyperactivity: an increase in the [[grafo/termos/synaptic strength|synaptic strength]] of PFn terminals onto ChI. In vivo data support the importance of these findings, demonstrating that the chemogenetic suppression of ChIs or PFn neurons, or knockdown of α6 messenger RNA, reverses [[grafo/termos/motor learning|motor-learning]] deficits in 6-OHDA mice. In addition to defining a novel maladaptive pathway that disrupts the dSPN/iSPN balance, this work points to α6-containing nAChRs and downstream signaling events as a potential [[grafo/termos/therapeutic target|therapeutic target]] for PD. The mechanism underlying the increased strength of PFn synapses onto ChIs remains unknown and will be an important goal for future studies. Considered broadly, this work emphasizes the importance of considering the effects of [[grafo/termos/dopamine depletion|dopamine depletion]] on the wider striatal network to identify novel molecular targets for PD therapeutics.
