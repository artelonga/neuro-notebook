Movement suppression in Parkinson's disease (PD) is thought to arise from increased efficacy of the [[grafo/termos/indirect pathway|indirect pathway]] [[grafo/termos/basal ganglia circuit|basal ganglia circuit]], relative to the [[grafo/termos/direct pathway|direct pathway]]. However, the underlying [[grafo/termos/pathophysiological mechanisms|pathophysiological mechanisms]] remain elusive. To examine whether changes in the strength of synaptic inputs to these circuits contribute to this imbalance, we obtained paired whole-cell recordings from striatal [[grafo/termos/direct and indirect pathways|direct- and indirect-pathway]] [[grafo/termos/medium spiny neurons|medium spiny neurons]] (dMSNs and iMSNs) and optically stimulated inputs from sensorimotor cortex or intralaminar [[grafo/termos/thalamus|thalamus]] in [[grafo/termos/brain slices|brain slices]] from control and dopamine-depleted mice. We found that [[grafo/termos/dopamine depletion|dopamine depletion]] selectively decreased [[grafo/termos/synaptic strength|synaptic strength]] at thalamic inputs to dMSNs, suggesting that thalamus drives asymmetric activation of [[grafo/termos/basal ganglia circuitry|basal ganglia circuitry]] underlying [[grafo/termos/parkinsonian motor|parkinsonian motor]] impairments. Consistent with this hypothesis, in vivo [[grafo/termos/chemogenetics|chemogenetic]] and optogenetic inhibition of thalamostriatal terminals reversed [[grafo/termos/motor deficits|motor deficits]] in dopamine-depleted mice. These results implicate thalamostriatal projections in the pathophysiology of PD and support interventions targeting thalamus as a potential [[grafo/termos/therapeutic strategy|therapeutic strategy]].
