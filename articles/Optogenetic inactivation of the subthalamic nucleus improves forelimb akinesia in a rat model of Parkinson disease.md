BACKGROUND: The inhibition of [[grafo/termos/neuronal activity|neuronal activity]] by electrical [[grafo/termos/deep brain stimulation|deep brain stimulation]] is one of the mechanisms explaining the therapeutic effects in patients with Parkinson disease (PD) but cannot specifically activate or inactivate different types of neurons. Recently, a new technology based on optogenetics has been developed to modulate the activity of [[grafo/termos/specific neurons|specific neurons]]. However, the therapeutic effects of optical inactivation in the [[grafo/termos/subthalamic nucleus|subthalamic nucleus]] (STN) have not been fully investigated. OBJECTIVE: To perform various behavioral tests to evaluate changes in [[grafo/termos/motor function|motor functions]] in a PD [[grafo/termos/rat model|rat model]] after optogene expression and, unlike previous studies, to assess the therapeutic effects of direct optogenetic inactivation in the STN. METHODS: 6-Hydroxydopamine-induced hemiparkinsonian rats received injections of hSynapsin1-NpHR-YFP [[grafo/termos/adeno-associated virus|adeno-associated virus]] or an equivalent volume of phosphate-buffered saline. Three weeks after injection of adeno-associated virus or phosphate-buffered saline, the optic fiber was implanted into the ipsilateral STN. A stepping test, a cylinder test, and an apomorphine-induced rotation test were performed in 3 sequential steps: during light-off state, during [[grafo/termos/light stimulation|light stimulation]], and again during light-off state. RESULTS: Stepping tests revealed that optical inhibition of the STN significantly improved 6-hydroxydopamine-induced forelimb [[grafo/termos/akinesia|akinesia]]. PD motor signs, as assessed by cylinder and apomorphine tests, were not affected by optical inhibition. Immunofluorescence revealed that [[grafo/termos/halorhodopsin|halorhodopsin]] was highly expressed and colocalized with vesicular [[grafo/termos/glutamate|glutamate]] transporter 2 in the STN. CONCLUSION: Optogenetic inhibition in the STN may be effective in improving contralateral forelimb akinesia but not in changing forelimb preference or reducing dopaminergic receptor supersensitivity. These findings are useful as a basis for future studies on optogenetics in PD.
