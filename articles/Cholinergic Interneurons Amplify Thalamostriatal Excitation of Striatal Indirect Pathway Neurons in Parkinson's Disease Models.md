The [[grafo/termos/motor symptoms|motor symptoms]] of Parkinson's disease (PD) are thought to stem from an imbalance in the activity of striatal [[grafo/termos/direct and indirect pathways|direct- and indirect-pathway]] [[grafo/termos/spiny projection neurons|spiny projection neurons]] (SPNs). Disease-induced alterations in the activity of networks controlling SPNs could contribute to this imbalance. One of these networks is anchored by the [[grafo/termos/parafascicular nucleus|parafascicular nucleus]] (PFn) of the [[grafo/termos/thalamus|thalamus]]. To determine the role of the PFn in striatal PD pathophysiology, optogenetic, [[grafo/termos/chemogenetics|chemogenetic]], and electrophysiological tools were used in ex vivo slices from transgenic mice with region-specific Cre recombinase expression. These studies revealed that in parkinsonian mice, the [[grafo/termos/functional connectivity|functional connectivity]] of PFn neurons with [[grafo/termos/indirect pathway|indirect pathway]] SPNs (iSPNs) was selectively enhanced by [[grafo/termos/cholinergic interneurons|cholinergic interneurons]] acting through presynaptic [[grafo/termos/nicotinic acetylcholine receptors|nicotinic acetylcholine receptors]] (nAChRs) on PFn terminals. Attenuating this network adaptation by chemogenetic or genetic strategies alleviated [[grafo/termos/motor learning|motor-learning]] deficits in parkinsonian mice, pointing to a potential new [[grafo/termos/therapeutic strategy|therapeutic strategy]] for PD patients.
