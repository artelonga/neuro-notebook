Brain [[grafo/termos/neuromodulatory systems|neuromodulatory systems]] exert powerful effects on local [[grafo/termos/neuronal circuits|neuronal circuit]] function and behavior. In addition to classical actions directly on neurons, growing [[grafo/termos/evidence indicates|evidence indicates]] that neuromodulators also recruit Ca2+-dependent [[grafo/termos/astrocytes|astrocyte]] mechanisms to regulate [[grafo/termos/synaptic plasticity|synaptic plasticity]] and network function. The [[grafo/termos/dorsal striatum|dorsal striatum]] integrates cortical and thalamic inputs under strong [[grafo/termos/dopamine|dopamine]] (DA) and [[grafo/termos/acetylcholine|acetylcholine]] (ACh) neuromodulatory control. To what extent the circuit and behavioral effects of striatal ACh and DA depend on astrocyte Ca2+ activity [[grafo/termos/remains unclear|remains unclear]]. We show that [[grafo/termos/locomotion|locomotion]] elicits robust DA, ACh, and astrocyte Ca2+ activity in the [[grafo/termos/dorsolateral striatum|dorsolateral striatum]] (DLS). DA and ACh release exhibits a negative correlation on a fast time scale but shows a positive correlation during continuous locomotion as slower astrocyte Ca2+ activity builds. Higher ACh and DA release is associated with higher astrocyte events, suggesting that both neurotransmitters drive astrocyte activity. In agreement, pharmacological blockade of muscarinic ACh or D1/D2 DA receptors decreases locomotion-evoked astrocyte Ca2+. Closed-loop optogenetic inhibition of [[grafo/termos/striatal cholinergic interneurons|striatal cholinergic interneurons]] (CINs) during locomotion reduces astrocyte Ca2+ activity, demonstrating a causal contribution of ACh release to astrocyte activity. Locomotion related ACh release was severely compromised in a mouse model of Parkinson's disease (PD), with the dual loss of DA and ACh attenuating astrocyte Ca2+ activity. Facilitating astrocyte [[grafo/termos/cholinergic signaling|cholinergic signaling]] via [[grafo/termos/chemogenetics|chemogenetics]] improved both calcium activity and [[grafo/termos/motor deficits|motor deficits]] in our recent work. Thus, the pathophysiology of PD in part involves attenuated astrocyte Ca2+ signaling, placing these non-neuronal cells as a prime underexplored [[grafo/termos/therapeutic target|therapeutic target]] for PD.
