Will it be possible in the future for blind people to see again using genes from light-sensitive algae ?Will epilepsy and Parkinson ' s disease soon be curable ?A conference which took place from September 2 -5, 2012 at the Free University of Berlin saw the pioneers of optogenetics from biophysics, biochemistry, neuroscience, genetics, and clinical neurology came together -among them Peter Hegemann, Professor of Experimental Biophysics at the Humboldt University in Berlin and one of the founders of optogenetics, and Prof. Roger Y. Tsien, a cell biologist at the University of California at San Diego, and Nobel Prize winner in Chemistry in 2008 -to discuss the realistic opportunities and potential risks of this rapidly developing field of research.Optogene tics combines methods of optics and genetics to control genetically modified cells with light and is based on an almost universal biological principle: Plants have photoreceptors like those that are found in the eyes of animals and humans.Such light-sensitive receptors can be incorporated into other " blind " cells where they function as a kind of light switch in the cell, triggering certain processes in the cell when irradiated with light.In this way, scientists can control cell activity from the outside with light.The advantage of this method is that light can be controlled very precisely, both in time and space, thereby allowing researchers to penetrate living tissue with pinpoint accuracy and without damaging it.Undesirable side effects can be avoided because adjacent cells are not affected.The first experiments have already been carried out using light-sensitive genes from algae to ' remotely ' control neurons of mice, worms and zebra fish.The conference was organized by Prof.
