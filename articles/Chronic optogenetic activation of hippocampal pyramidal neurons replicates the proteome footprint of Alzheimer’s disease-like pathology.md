Neuronal overexcitability can elicit synaptic changes, leading to [[grafo/termos/neuronal hyperactivity|neuronal hyperactivity]] and abnormal [[grafo/termos/neural circuits|neural circuit]] processing. Such [[grafo/termos/network disruption|network disruption]] impairs [[grafo/termos/neuronal functions|neuronal function]] and survival, initiating [[grafo/termos/neurodegeneration|neurodegeneration]] and Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Yet, the sequence of synaptic changes resulting from excessive [[grafo/termos/neuronal activity|neuronal activity]] remains elusive. We employed optogenetics to induce sustained neuronal hyperactivity in the hippocampi of wild-type and AD-like 5xFAD mice. Surprisingly, after a month of daily optogenetic stimulation, the proteomic profiles of photoactivated wild-type and 5xFAD mice exhibited remarkable similarity. Proteins involved in translation, protein transport, [[grafo/termos/autophagy|autophagy]], and notably in the AD pathology were upregulated in wild-type mice. Conversely, both glutamatergic and GABAergic [[grafo/termos/synaptic proteins|synaptic proteins]] were downregulated. These hippocampal proteomic and signaling alterations in wild-type mice resulted in [[grafo/termos/spatial memory|spatial memory]] loss and augmented Αβ42 secretion. Collectively, these findings indicate that sustained neuronal hyperactivity alone replicates proteome changes seen in AD-linked mutant mice. Therefore, prolonged neuronal hyperactivity may contribute to [[grafo/termos/synaptic transmission|synaptic transmission]] disruption, [[grafo/termos/memory deficits|memory deficits]] and the neurodegenerative process associated with AD.
