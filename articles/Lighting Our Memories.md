As a boy, Dheeraj Roy spent summers in India with his paternal grandmother.It was a happy time, filled with camping, hiking, and delicious food-a time that his grandmother would also recall with fondness years later.But by 2012, things had changed.Roy, newly installed as a graduate student at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, went back to India for a visit with his parents and grandmother.He brought gifts-food and clothing from the United States-and was anxious to reminisce about the past.But when he saw his grandmother, he says, "she didn't know who I was."Roy's grandmother was suffering from Alzheimers, a disease that robs its victims first of their past and then of their dignity.There is no cure, nor even a reliable antemortem diagnostic for the disease.For Roy, it was a galvanizing moment, spurring him to design a graduate research project that he hopes might one day lead to advances that could spare others from his grandmother's fate: "That experience is on my mind every day."Fortunately, Roy was in the right lab to make that happen.MIT professor Susumu Tonegawa, a 1987 Nobel laureate for his work on how B cells are able to generate a seemingly infinite variety of antibody designs, has been focusing on neuroscience, specifically memory and learning, since the 1990s.Roy was now a student in Tonegawa's lab, and they were making headway in understanding the brain using a powerful neuroscience tool: optogenetics.
