The [[grafo/termos/basal ganglia|basal ganglia]] are a network of interconnected subcortical nuclei that integrate signals from cortical and subcortical regions to regulate a [[grafo/termos/wide range|wide range]] of behaviors, including motor, cognitive, motivational, and emotional processes. [[grafo/termos/dopamine|Dopamine]] is a key modulator within these circuits, and its dysregulation is implicated in both neurodegenerative and neurodevelopmental disorders. However, the precise mechanisms through which dopamine regulates [[grafo/termos/synaptic transmission|synaptic transmission]] and how it both influences and is influenced by other [[grafo/termos/neurotransmitter systems|neurotransmitter systems]] remain incompletely understood. The work presented in this thesis aims to gain a better understanding of these mechanisms in both physiological and pathological conditions. In Paper I, the goal was to investigate how dopamine modulates [[grafo/termos/gaba release|GABA release]] into the [[grafo/termos/substantia nigra pars reticulata|substantia nigra pars reticulata]] (SNr), a major [[grafo/termos/basal ganglia output|basal ganglia output]] nucleus, in the healthy brain. Because [[grafo/termos/dopamine d1 receptor|dopamine D1 receptors]] are densely expressed in striatal terminals within the SNr, we hypothesized that they might mediate dopamine's effects on striatonigral transmission. Using ex vivo optical and electrophysiological approaches, we found that dopamine reduces striatonigral GABA release in a frequency-dependent manner, particularly during low- frequency transmission, consistent with a high-pass filtering mechanism at these [[grafo/termos/synapse|synapses]]. Despite the high expression of D1 receptors at striatonigral terminals, we unexpectedly found that this effect was not mediated by [[grafo/termos/dopamine receptors|dopamine receptors]]. Instead, it required activation of serotonin (5-HT)1B receptors. Importantly, we demonstrated that dopamine induces serotonin efflux through inhibition of serotonin reuptake, thereby activating presynaptic 5-HT1B receptors. Together, these results demonstrate an important interaction between dopamine and serotonin that ultimately influences basal ganglia output and may have meaningful implications for behavior. In Paper II, we investigated how chronic dopamine loss in Parkinson's disease (PD) affects [[grafo/termos/gabaergic inhibition|GABAergic inhibition]] within the SNr, using a [[grafo/termos/6-hydroxydopamine|6-hydroxydopamine]] (6- OHDA) mouse model. PD is characterized by the degeneration of [[grafo/termos/dopaminergic neurons|dopaminergic neurons]] in the [[grafo/termos/substantia nigra pars compacta|substantia nigra pars compacta]] (SNc), which leads not only to reduced dopamine levels throughout the basal ganglia but also to synaptic and circuit-level adaptations. The SNr exhibits abnormal [[grafo/termos/firing patterns|firing patterns]] associated with PD-like [[grafo/termos/motor deficits|motor deficits]], yet the underlying synaptic mechanisms remain [[grafo/termos/poorly understood|poorly understood]]. Using optogenetic stimulation of striatal and pallidonigral GABAergic inputs to the SNr combined with [[grafo/termos/electrophysiological recordings|electrophysiological recordings]] in [[grafo/termos/brain slices|brain slices]] from 6-OHDA-lesioned mice, we found that chronic [[grafo/termos/dopamine depletion|dopamine depletion]] weakens inhibitory control from both pathways onto SNr neurons. Interestingly, GABAA receptor-mediated currents were not altered, indicating preserved postsynaptic receptor function. Instead, impaired inhibition was associated with reduced membrane expression of the potassium-chloride co- transporter KCC2, leading to impaired chloride extrusion. Importantly, pharmacological enhancement of KCC2 restored chloride extrusion and improved motor performance in parkinsonian mice. These findings identify altered chloride regulation and impaired GABAergic inhibition as important mechanisms contributing to SNr dysfunction and suggest KCC2 enhancement as a potential [[grafo/termos/therapeutic strategy|therapeutic strategy]] in PD. Finally, in Paper III, we investigated [[grafo/termos/striatal dopamine|striatal dopamine]] neurotransmission in the eIF4E mouse model of [[grafo/termos/autism spectrum disorder|autism spectrum disorders]] (ASD), characterized by exaggerated protein translation. Altered [[grafo/termos/dopamine signaling|dopamine signaling]] has, in fact, been proposed to contribute to key behavioral features of the disorder. Using a combination of behavioral analysis, synaptic physiology, and imaging approaches, we found that increased eIF4E expression leads to behavioral inflexibility accompanied by impaired [[grafo/termos/striatal dopamine release|striatal dopamine release]]. This deficit was due to altered [[grafo/termos/nicotinic receptors|nicotinic receptor]] signaling on dopaminergic axons. These findings link dysregulated [[grafo/termos/protein synthesis|protein synthesis]] to impaired cholinergic control of [[grafo/termos/dopamine transmission|dopamine transmission]] and suggest that altered striatal dopamine signaling may contribute to behavioral inflexibility in ASD. Altogether, these studies provide a better understanding of how dopamine interacts with other neurotransmitter systems to modulate synaptic transmission and shape basal ganglia activity in both health and disease. They also highlight the complexity of dopaminergic regulation and how its disruption can profoundly affect circuit function and behavior. List of scientific papers I.
