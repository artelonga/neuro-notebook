The basal ganglia are a network of interconnected subcortical nuclei that integrate signals from cortical and subcortical regions to regulate a wide range of behaviors, including motor, cognitive, motivational, and emotional processes. Dopamine is a key modulator within these circuits, and its dysregulation is implicated in both neurodegenerative and neurodevelopmental disorders. However, the precise mechanisms through which dopamine regulates synaptic transmission and how it both influences and is influenced by other neurotransmitter systems remain incompletely understood. The work presented in this thesis aims to gain a better understanding of these mechanisms in both physiological and pathological conditions. In Paper I, the goal was to investigate how dopamine modulates GABA release into the substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNr), a major basal ganglia output nucleus, in the healthy brain. Because dopamine D1 receptors are densely expressed in striatal terminals within the SNr, we hypothesized that they might mediate dopamine's effects on striatonigral transmission. Using ex vivo optical and electrophysiological approaches, we found that dopamine reduces striatonigral GABA release in a frequency-dependent manner, particularly during low- frequency transmission, consistent with a high-pass filtering mechanism at these synapses. Despite the high expression of D1 receptors at striatonigral terminals, we unexpectedly found that this effect was not mediated by dopamine receptors. Instead, it required activation of serotonin (5-HT)1B receptors. Importantly, we demonstrated that dopamine induces serotonin efflux through inhibition of serotonin reuptake, thereby activating presynaptic 5-HT1B receptors. Together, these results demonstrate an important interaction between dopamine and serotonin that ultimately influences basal ganglia output and may have meaningful implications for behavior. In Paper II, we investigated how chronic dopamine loss in Parkinson's disease (PD) affects GABAergic inhibition within the SNr, using a 6-hydroxydopamine (6- OHDA) mouse model. PD is characterized by the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc), which leads not only to reduced dopamine levels throughout the basal ganglia but also to synaptic and circuit-level adaptations. The SNr exhibits abnormal firing patterns associated with PD-like motor deficits, yet the underlying synaptic mechanisms remain poorly understood. Using optogenetic stimulation of striatal and pallidonigral GABAergic inputs to the SNr combined with electrophysiological recordings in brain slices from 6-OHDA-lesioned mice, we found that chronic dopamine depletion weakens inhibitory control from both pathways onto SNr neurons. Interestingly, GABAA receptor-mediated currents were not altered, indicating preserved postsynaptic receptor function. Instead, impaired inhibition was associated with reduced membrane expression of the potassium-chloride co- transporter KCC2, leading to impaired chloride extrusion. Importantly, pharmacological enhancement of KCC2 restored chloride extrusion and improved motor performance in parkinsonian mice. These findings identify altered chloride regulation and impaired GABAergic inhibition as important mechanisms contributing to SNr dysfunction and suggest KCC2 enhancement as a potential therapeutic strategy in PD. Finally, in Paper III, we investigated striatal dopamine neurotransmission in the eIF4E mouse model of autism spectrum disorders (ASD), characterized by exaggerated protein translation. Altered dopamine signaling has, in fact, been proposed to contribute to key behavioral features of the disorder. Using a combination of behavioral analysis, synaptic physiology, and imaging approaches, we found that increased eIF4E expression leads to behavioral inflexibility accompanied by impaired striatal dopamine release. This deficit was due to altered nicotinic receptor signaling on dopaminergic axons. These findings link dysregulated protein synthesis to impaired cholinergic control of dopamine transmission and suggest that altered striatal dopamine signaling may contribute to behavioral inflexibility in ASD. Altogether, these studies provide a better understanding of how dopamine interacts with other neurotransmitter systems to modulate synaptic transmission and shape basal ganglia activity in both health and disease.
