Parkinson's disease (PD) is a [[grafo/termos/neurodegenerative disorders|neurodegenerative disorder]] caused by the loss of nigral [[grafo/termos/dopaminergic neurons|dopaminergic neurons]] innervating the [[grafo/termos/striatum|striatum]], the main input structure of the [[grafo/termos/basal ganglia|basal ganglia]]. This creates an imbalance between dopaminergic inputs and [[grafo/termos/cholinergic interneurons|cholinergic interneurons]] (ChIs) within the striatum. The efficacy of anticholinergic drugs, one of the earliest therapy for PD before the discovery of L-3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine ([[grafo/termos/l-dopa|L-DOPA]]) suggests an increased cholinergic tone in this disease. The [[grafo/termos/dopamine|dopamine]] (DA)-acetylcholine (ACh) balance hypothesis is now revisited with the use of novel cutting-edge techniques (optogenetics, pharmacogenetics, new [[grafo/termos/electrophysiological recordings|electrophysiological recordings]]). This review will provide the background of the specific contribution of ChIs to striatal microcircuit organization in physiological and pathological conditions. The second goal of this review is to delve into the respective contributions of nicotinic and [[grafo/termos/muscarinic receptors|muscarinic receptor]] cholinergic subunits to the control of striatal afferent and efferent neuronal systems. Special attention will be given to the role played by [[grafo/termos/muscarinic acetylcholine receptor|muscarinic acetylcholine receptors]] (mAChRs) in the regulation of striatal network which may have important implications in the development of novel [[grafo/termos/therapeutic strategies|therapeutic strategies]] for motor and [[grafo/termos/cognitive impairment|cognitive impairment]] in PD.
