[[grafo/termos/deep brain stimulation|Deep brain stimulation]] of the [[grafo/termos/subthalamic nucleus|subthalamic nucleus]] is a symptomatic treatment of Parkinson’s disease but benefits only to a minority of patients due to stringent eligibility criteria. To investigate new targets for less invasive therapies, we aimed at elucidating key mechanisms supporting deep brain stimulation efficiency. Here, using in [[grafo/termos/vivo electrophysiology|vivo electrophysiology]], optogenetics and modeling, we found that subthalamic stimulation normalizes pathological hyperactivity of [[grafo/termos/motor cortex|motor cortex]] [[grafo/termos/pyramidal cells|pyramidal cells]], while concurrently activating somatostatin and inhibiting [[grafo/termos/parvalbumin interneurons|parvalbumin interneurons]]. In vivo opto-activation of cortical [[grafo/termos/somatostatin interneurons|somatostatin interneurons]] alleviates [[grafo/termos/motor symptoms|motor symptoms]] in a parkinsonian mouse model. A mathematical model highlights how the decrease in [[grafo/termos/pyramidal neurons|pyramidal neurons]] activity can restore [[grafo/termos/information processing|information processing]] capabilities. Overall, these results demonstrate that activation of cortical somatostatin interneurons may constitute a less invasive alternative than subthalamic stimulation. One-sentence Summary Deep brain stimulation recruits cortical somatostatin interneurons, and their opto-activation is beneficial in Parkinson’s.
