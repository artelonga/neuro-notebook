The brain&s;s process of learning and [[grafo/termos/memory formation|memory formation]] is primarily driven by [[grafo/termos/synaptic plasticity|synaptic plasticity]], which is the capacity of [[grafo/termos/synapse|synapses]] to become stronger or weaker over time in response to activity. This phenomenon involves complex molecular and cellular pathways and encompasses both [[grafo/termos/long-term depression|long-term depression]] (LTD) and [[grafo/termos/long-term potentiation|long-term potentiation]] (LTP). The function of neurotransmitters, [[grafo/termos/ion channels|ion channels]], and intracellular signaling cascades are highlighted in this chapter, which examines molecular and [[grafo/termos/cellular mechanisms|cellular mechanism]] driven synaptic plasticity. This chapter also explores the various uses of synaptic plasticity research. Knowing synaptic plasticity is important because it can help treat [[grafo/termos/neurological disorders|neurological disorders]] like [[grafo/termos/multiple sclerosis|multiple sclerosis]], Parkinson&s;s disease, and Alzheimer&s;s disease, where impaired synapse function leads to [[grafo/termos/cognitive decline|cognitive decline]]. Later on in this chapter, different techniques such as optogenetics and [[grafo/termos/chemogenetics|chemogenetics]] through which researchers are trying to monitor these modulations in synapse functions, are also discussed. So by elucidating the intricate interplay of neurotransmitters, receptors, and [[grafo/termos/signaling pathways|signaling pathways]], researchers can harness synaptic plasticity to unlock new therapeutic avenues and improve [[grafo/termos/cognitive function|cognitive function]] and quality of life.
