Recent studies have shown evidence of behavioral recovery after transplantation of human [[grafo/termos/pluripotent stem cells|pluripotent stem cell]] (PSC)-derived neural cells in animal models of neurological disease. However, little is known about the [[grafo/termos/mechanisms underlying|mechanisms underlying]] graft function. Here we use optogenetics to modulate in real time electrophysiological and neurochemical properties of mesencephalic dopaminergic (mesDA) [[grafo/termos/neurons derived|neurons derived]] from human [[grafo/termos/embryonic stem cells|embryonic stem cells]] (hESCs). In mice that had recovered from lesion-induced [[grafo/termos/parkinsonian motor|Parkinsonian motor]] deficits, light-induced selective silencing of graft activity rapidly and reversibly re-introduced the [[grafo/termos/motor deficits|motor deficits]]. The re-introduction of motor deficits was prevented by the [[grafo/termos/dopamine|dopamine]] agonist apomorphine. These results suggest that functionality depends on graft [[grafo/termos/neuronal activity|neuronal activity]] and [[grafo/termos/dopamine release|dopamine release]]. Combining optogenetics, slice [[grafo/termos/electrophysiology|electrophysiology]] and pharmacological approaches, we further show that mesDA-rich grafts modulate host glutamatergic [[grafo/termos/synaptic transmission|synaptic transmission]] onto [[grafo/termos/striatal medium spiny neurons|striatal medium spiny neurons]] in a manner reminiscent of endogenous mesDA neurons. Thus, application of optogenetics in [[grafo/termos/cell therapy|cell therapy]] can link transplantation, animal behavior and postmortem analysis to enable the identification of mechanisms that drive recovery.
