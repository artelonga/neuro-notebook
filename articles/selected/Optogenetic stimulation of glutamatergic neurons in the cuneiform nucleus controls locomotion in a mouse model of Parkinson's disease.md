In Parkinson's disease (PD), the loss of midbrain [[grafo/termos/dopaminergic cells|dopaminergic cells]] results in severe [[grafo/termos/locomotor deficits|locomotor deficits]], such as gait freezing and [[grafo/termos/akinesia|akinesia]]. Growing [[grafo/termos/evidence indicates|evidence indicates]] that these deficits can be attributed to the decreased activity in the [[grafo/termos/mesencephalic locomotor region|mesencephalic locomotor region]] (MLR), a [[grafo/termos/brainstem|brainstem]] region controlling [[grafo/termos/locomotion|locomotion]]. Clinicians are exploring the [[grafo/termos/deep brain stimulation|deep brain stimulation]] of the MLR as a [[grafo/termos/treatment options|treatment option]] to improve locomotor function. The results are variable, from modest to promising. However, within the MLR, clinicians have targeted the [[grafo/termos/pedunculopontine nucleus|pedunculopontine nucleus]] exclusively, while leaving the [[grafo/termos/cuneiform nucleus|cuneiform nucleus]] unexplored. To our knowledge, the effects of cuneiform nucleus stimulation have never been determined in [[grafo/termos/parkinsonian conditions|parkinsonian conditions]] in any animal model. Here, we addressed this issue in a mouse model of PD, based on the bilateral striatal injection of [[grafo/termos/6-hydroxydopamine|6-hydroxydopamine]], which damaged the [[grafo/termos/nigrostriatal pathway|nigrostriatal pathway]] and decreased [[grafo/termos/locomotor activity|locomotor activity]]. We show that selective optogenetic stimulation of [[grafo/termos/glutamatergic neurons|glutamatergic neurons]] in the cuneiform nucleus in mice expressing [[grafo/termos/channelrhodopsin|channelrhodopsin]] in a Cre-dependent manner in Vglut2-positive neurons (Vglut2-ChR2-EYFP mice) increased the number of locomotor initiations, increased the time spent in locomotion, and controlled locomotor speed. Using deep learning-based movement analysis, we found that the limb kinematics of optogenetic-evoked locomotion in pathological conditions were largely similar to those recorded in intact animals. Our work identifies the glutamatergic neurons of the cuneiform nucleus as a potentially clinically relevant target to improve locomotor activity in parkinsonian conditions. Our study should open avenues to develop the targeted stimulation of these neurons using deep brain stimulation, pharmacotherapy, or optogenetics.
