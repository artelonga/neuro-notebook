The classic [[grafo/termos/basal ganglia circuit|basal ganglia circuit]] model asserts a complete segregation of the two striatal output pathways. Empirical data argue that, in addition to [[grafo/termos/indirect pathway|indirect-pathway]] [[grafo/termos/striatal projection neurons|striatal projection neurons]] (iSPNs), [[grafo/termos/direct pathway|direct-pathway]] striatal projection neurons (dSPNs) innervate the [[grafo/termos/external globus pallidus|external globus pallidus]] (GPe). However, the functions of the latter were not known. In this study, we interrogated the organization principles of striatopallidal projections and their roles in full-body movement in mice (both males and females). In contrast to the canonical motor-promoting response of dSPNs in the [[grafo/termos/dorsomedial striatum|dorsomedial striatum]] (DMSdSPNs), optogenetic stimulation of dSPNs in the [[grafo/termos/dorsolateral striatum|dorsolateral striatum]] (DLSdSPNs) suppressed [[grafo/termos/locomotion|locomotion]]. Circuit analyses revealed that dSPNs selectively target Npas1+ neurons in the GPe. In a chronic [[grafo/termos/6-hydroxydopamine lesion|6-hydroxydopamine lesion]] model of Parkinson's disease, the dSPN-Npas1+ projection was dramatically strengthened. As DLSdSPN-Npas1+ projection suppresses movement, the enhancement of this projection represents a [[grafo/termos/circuit mechanisms|circuit mechanism]] for the hypokinetic symptoms of Parkinson's disease that has not been previously considered. In sum, our results suggest that dSPN input to the GPe is a critical circuit component that is involved in the regulation of movement in both healthy and [[grafo/termos/parkinsonian states|parkinsonian states]].SIGNIFICANCE STATEMENT In the classic [[grafo/termos/basal ganglia|basal ganglia]] model, the [[grafo/termos/striatum|striatum]] is described as a divergent structure: it controls motor and adaptive functions through two segregated, opposing output streams. However, the experimental results that show the projection from direct-pathway neurons to the external pallidum have been largely ignored. Here, we showed that this striatopallidal subpathway targets a select subset of neurons in the external pallidum and is motor-suppressing. We found that this subpathway undergoes changes in a Parkinson's disease model. In particular, our results suggest that the increase in strength of this subpathway contributes to the slowness or reduced movements observed in Parkinson's disease.
