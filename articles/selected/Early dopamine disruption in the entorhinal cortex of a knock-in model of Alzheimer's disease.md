The [[grafo/termos/entorhinal cortex|entorhinal cortex]] is a critical [[grafo/termos/brain areas|brain area]] for [[grafo/termos/memory formation|memory formation]], while also the region exhibiting the earliest histological and [[grafo/termos/functional alterations|functional alterations]] in Alzheimer's disease (AD). The entorhinal cortex therefore has been long hypothesized as one of the originating brain areas of AD pathophysiology, although [[grafo/termos/circuit mechanisms|circuit mechanisms]] causing its selective vulnerability remain [[grafo/termos/poorly understood|poorly understood]]. Here we show that [[grafo/termos/dopamine neurons|dopamine neurons]] projecting their axons to the lateral entorhinal cortex (LEC), critical for memory formation in healthy brains, become dysfunctional from the early pathological stage and cause associative [[grafo/termos/memory impairment|memory impairments]] in [[grafo/termos/amyloid precursor protein|amyloid precursor protein]] knock-in mice. [[grafo/termos/dopamine|Dopamine]] dysfunction led to the disruption of associative [[grafo/termos/memory|memory]] encoding of LEC layer 2/3. Optogenetic reactivation of LEC dopamine fibers rescued associative learning behavior. [[grafo/termos/l-dopa treatment|L-DOPA treatment]] restored memory encoding of LEC neurons and associative memory of amyloid precursor protein knock-in mice. These results suggest early dysfunction of LEC-projecting dopamine neurons underlie memory impairment in AD from [[grafo/termos/early stages|early stages]], pointing to a need for clinical investigation of LEC dopamine in patients with AD.
