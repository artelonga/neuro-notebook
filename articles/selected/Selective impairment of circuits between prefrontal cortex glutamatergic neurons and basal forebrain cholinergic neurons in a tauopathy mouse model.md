Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a prevalent [[grafo/termos/neurodegenerative disorders|neurodegenerative disorder]] linked to [[grafo/termos/cognitive decline|cognitive decline]]. To understand how specific [[grafo/termos/neuronal circuits|neuronal circuits]] are impaired in AD, we have used optogenetic and electrophysiological approaches to reveal the functional changes between [[grafo/termos/prefrontal cortex|prefrontal cortex]] (PFC) and [[grafo/termos/basal forebrain|basal forebrain]] (BF), 2 key regions controlling cognitive processes, in a [[grafo/termos/tauopathy|tauopathy]] mouse model. We found that the glutamatergic synaptic responses in BF [[grafo/termos/cholinergic neurons|cholinergic neurons]] from [[grafo/termos/p301s tau|P301S Tau]] mice (6-8 months old) were markedly diminished. The attenuated long-range PFC to BF pathway in the AD model significantly increased the failure rate of [[grafo/termos/action potentials|action potential]] firing of BF cholinergic neurons triggered by optogenetic stimulations of glutamatergic terminals from PFC. In contrast, the projection from PFC to other regions, such as amygdala and [[grafo/termos/striatum|striatum]], was largely unaltered. On the other hand, optogenetic stimulation of cholinergic terminals from BF induced a persistent reduction of the excitability of PFC [[grafo/termos/pyramidal neurons|pyramidal neurons]] from [[grafo/termos/tau|Tau]] mice, instead of the transient reduction exhibited in wild-type mice. Taken together, these data have revealed a selective aberration of the pathway between PFC pyramidal neurons and BF cholinergic neurons in a tauopathy mouse model. This circuit deficit may underlie the loss of attention and [[grafo/termos/executive function|executive function]] in AD.
