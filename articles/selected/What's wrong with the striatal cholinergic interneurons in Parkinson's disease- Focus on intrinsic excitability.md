Parkinson's disease (PD) is characterized by a degeneration of nigrostriatal [[grafo/termos/dopaminergic neurons|dopaminergic neurons]] that results in a hypercholinergic state in the [[grafo/termos/striatum|striatum]]. This hypercholinergic state contributes to the clinical signs of PD. However, the mechanisms that underlie this state remain unknown. [[grafo/termos/cholinergic interneurons|Cholinergic interneurons]] (ChIs) are the main source of [[grafo/termos/acetylcholine|acetylcholine]] in the striatum. Many studies have highlighted the importance of their normal physiological activity to guarantee a normal [[grafo/termos/motor control|motor control]] and goal-directed behaviour. Moreover, recent studies with optogenetic and [[grafo/termos/chemogenetics|chemogenetic]] approaches have shown that reducing ChIs activity ameliorates [[grafo/termos/parkinsonian symptoms|parkinsonian symptoms]] and modifies [[grafo/termos/l-dopa-induced dyskinesia|L-dopa induced dyskinesia]] in PD animal models. Here, we review the described alterations in ChIs physiology that may contribute to a hypercholinergic state in PD. The best-established finding is an increase of ChIs intrinsic membrane excitability after dopaminergic denervation of striatum. Understanding the [[grafo/termos/molecular basis|molecular basis]] of ChIs dysfunction in PD could help to develop new therapeutic tools to restore their normal activity and decrease parkinsonian symptoms, improving life quality of PD patients.
