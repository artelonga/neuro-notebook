New directions in science are launched by new tools much more often than by new concepts. The effect of a concept-driven revolution is to explain old things in new ways. The effect of a tool-driven revolution is to discover new things that have to be explained. —Freeman Dyson From the modern perspective, it can be difficult to appreciate the degree to which basic cardiac research of three to four decades ago, including its nascent molecular biology component, was almost entirely observational. Key questions relating to cardiac development were unanswered: What proteins and genes are the signatures of cardiac muscle cell identity? When are they first expressed, which ones distinguish the atria from the ventricles, and what changes occur over time? Likewise, the molecular determinants of functional compensation in myocardial diseases, eliciting cardiac hypertrophy and provoking the subsequent transition to heart failure, were unknown. For many current investigators, defining the unique composition of cardiomyocytes and uncovering the surprising plasticity of myocardial gene expression in response to altered mechanical load became animating principles, driving subsequent cycles of mechanistic questions: what cis-acting DNA sequences, trans-acting DNA binding proteins, and regulatory signalling cascades orchestrate gene expression that primarily defines cardiac-specificity, and how are these programs modulated in disease? Definitive answers to these and many other questions required moving the research platforms beyond cell culture surrogates to the in vivo heart itself. Jeff Robbins, who died tragically this summer, is missed not just as an adroit colleague, gifted mentor, and endlessly amusing friend but also as one of the individuals who most transformed the capabilities of cardiac biology over the past 30-plus years. By pinpointing the information for cardiomyocyte-restricted transcription of α-myosin heavy chains (MHCs) (among the most stringently heart-specific proteins) to a 5.5 kb intergenic region between the mouse Myh6 and Myh7 genes,1 Jeff took the first step towards answering many of the foundational questions posed above, and also created one of the most powerful and widely adopted tools ever introduced into the field of molecular cardiology. The murine 5.5 kb Myh6 promoter, colloquially known as the ‘Robbins α-myosin heavy chain (MHC) promoter’ has been used in hundreds of research manuscripts to engineer transgenic mice with gain-of-function mutations, loss-of-function mutations, and the conditional (drug-induced) variations of both. An early focus of this technology was to create innovative models of hereditary heart disease in humans, including hypertrophic cardiomyopathy by Jeff and colleagues (MYBPC3, TNNI3), plus much work elsewhere on hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (TPM1), dilated cardiomyopathy (PLN) and Wolff–Parkinson–White syndrome (PRKAG2). Animal models like these—bearing the human mutations identified through linkage analysis and positional cloning—became a platform to demonstrate in vivo causal relations between human gene variations and cardiac phenotypes, and ultimately to serve as a testbed for experimental therapeutics designed to mitigate the consequences of these gene mutations. Another broad category of Jeff’s contributions as both an investigator and an indispensable resource involves using his 5.5 kb Myh6 promoter to perturb signalling pathways posited to evoke secondary changes of acquired heart disorders, thereby testing putative roles as pathobiological effectors, innocent bystanders, or compensatory responses. Examples of altered signalling cascade activity first elucidated using the Robbins α-MHC promoter include Gαq2 and calcineurin.3 Where a causative role has been substantiated, these models, like those built upon human mutations, have become part of a new paradigmatic route—defining downstream effects, developing novel countermeasures, or repurposing existing ones. An unusual example was Jeff’s discovery of a stress-induced 40 kDa fragment of Mybpc3 that suffices to trigger cardiac fibrosis and mortality, then showing therapeutic benefits by inhibiting the pro-fibrotic protein kinase MAPKAPK2 or ablating TGFβ receptor II signalling in myofibroblasts.4 This work was also noteworthy for his use of the Myh6 promoter to achieve temporally controlled, drug-inducible transgenesis exclusively in adult heats. The myriad translational applications engendered by myocardial perturbation using the Robbins α-MHC promoter include calcineurin itself, downstream proteins, dysregulated microRNAs, stress-induced cytokines, and alternative candidate drivers of transcriptional reprogramming, myocyte dysfunction, and myocyte death. Jeff’s own investigations using the Myh6 promoter region as an enabling technology also include sarcomeric proteins’ intricate structure-function relationships. He replaced the known phosphorylation sites in myosin binding protein C with non-phosphorylatable alanines or phosphomimetic aspartic acid residues, collectively and individually (a perhaps unequalled tour de force),5 highlighting the potential of transgenesis to explore fundamental structure-function relationships of cardiac proteins in their native environment. Jeff’s later interests came to focus largely on protein quality control via the ubiquitin proteasome system—heart failure as an unfolded protein response or ‘aggregopathy’—with Myh6-driven lines (desmin, alpha B-crystallin, calpains, proteasome 28 subunit α, and a preamyloid polyglutamine oligomer) all instrumental to establishing the scientific case.6 Beyond merely gain-of-function and missense mutations, using the 5.5 kb Myh6 promoter for cardiomyocyte-directed expression of Cre recombinase7 or a drug-dependent Cre fusion protein8 has been equally transformative, helping explicate cardiac biology through cardiomyocyte-specific knockouts, conditionally inducible cardiomyocyte-specific knockouts, cardiomyocyte-specific knockins, and novel lineage-tracing tools. Myh6-driven tool transgenic lines have also emerged, expressing ectopic proteins as cardiomyocyte-restricted biomarkers of cell division, autophagy, mitochondrial turnover, and other processes, and even as substrates for optogenetic manipulation of heart function. Development of a Myh6-driven CRISPR/Cas9 system for adult, heart-specific genome editing9 is likely to accelerate the future pace and increase the precision of genetic engineering for the heart. Likewise, Jeff’s early research with embryonic stem cell pioneer Tom Doetschman, revealing that embryoid bodies grown from these cells undergo rhythmic contractions and express both cardiac MHC genes, was an accessible model system that recapitulated aspects of cardiogenesis10 that anticipated current efforts to use stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes as experimental platforms for drug discovery, or as therapeutics in their own right. Few other tools in cardiac molecular biology—certainly, few bits of DNA—can lay claim to the real-world scientific impact of the Robbins 5.5 kb Myh6 promoter. While its applicability to diverse scientific questions is an intrinsic property of the construct, the ubiquity with which the α-MHC promoter has emerged as the go-to tool for cardiomyocyte-specific gene manipulation is directly attributable to the foresight and incredible generosity of its inventor. Jeff’s approach to research is summarized by the following: ‘Invent it, publish it, and then give it away.’ Rather than keep the α-MHC promoter in-house to advance his and his collaborators’ individual research programs, Jeff provided the construct to all who requested it, including those who might be considered competitors. By freely distributing his α-MHC promoter construct, Jeff Robbins accepted the risk that he would enable competing laboratories, in order to transform his chosen field. This ethos, shared by Jeff with his collaborators, trainees, and mentees over many years, has been adopted by a veritable Who’s Who of investigators, including his worthy successor at the helm of Cincinnati Children’s Hospital, Jeffery Molkentin. Thus, while we fondly remember Jeff Robbins for his many endearing personal attributes, including a penchant for black t-shirts, Porsches, and fine wine, his scientific legacy endures in the discoveries he made, the techniques he developed and shared, and the attitude he fostered . For many, he was a life coach. He is sorely missed.
