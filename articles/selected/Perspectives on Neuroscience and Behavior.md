Spontaneous hallucinations are experienced by over 5% of the general nonclinical population, and they are increased across a range of neurological and psychiatric illnesses (e.g., schizophrenia and [[grafo/termos/dopamine|dopamine]] agonist treatment of [[grafo/termos/parkinsonism|parkinsonism]]). Now, in an outstanding mechanistic study of “hallucinationlike percepts” (HALIPs) in mice, it has been found that increased dopaminergic activity in the [[grafo/termos/ventral striatum|ventral striatum]] (VS) and the tail of the [[grafo/termos/striatum|striatum]] (TS) correlated with increased HALIPs; optogenetic stimulation of the VS increased HALIPs, which were blocked by haloperidol; and using a similar behavioral task to that used in mice, HALIPs in 220 humans with self-reported proneness to hallucinations had a low but significant correlation with the experience of spontaneous hallucinations. In addition, ketamine at a psychotomimetic dose increased the false alarm rate in mice. HALIPs were measured in mice during an auditory signal detection task where the signal was embedded in varying levels of noise and were defined as “high-confidence false alarms.” In humans with a similar task, false alarms and false alarm confidence ratings were used to correlate with the proneness to hallucinations using the Cardiff Anomalous Perception Scale. A Bayesian [[grafo/termos/computational model|computational model]] accounted for the behavioral data in both humans and mice. It was found that VS dopaminergic activity reflected [[grafo/termos/reward|reward]] expectations while VS activity resembled perceptual expectations (Schmack and others 2021). A central question from these findings is, how well do sensory-induced hallucination-like percepts in mice engage the [[grafo/termos/neural mechanisms|neural mechanisms]] that are involved in spontaneously experienced hallucinations in humans? The [[grafo/termos/wide range|wide range]] of medical conditions with hallucinations in humans and the very low correlation between the sensory-induced HALIPs and the proneness to hallucinations in humans suggest that increased dopaminergic tone is an important modulator but not the sole mediator involving other overlapping circuits involved in hallucinations. However, these findings do provide important evidence to support the investigation of the role of increased dopaminergic function across a range of human illness with hallucinations and supports the investigation of [[grafo/termos/dopamine receptors|dopamine receptor]] blockers in different conditions to reduce hallucinations.
