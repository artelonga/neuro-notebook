The [[grafo/termos/locus coeruleus|locus coeruleus]] (LC) has long been underappreciated for its role in the pathophysiology of Alzheimer's disease (AD), Parkinson's disease (PD), and other [[grafo/termos/neurodegenerative disorders|neurodegenerative disorders]]. While AD and PD are distinct in clinical presentation, both are characterized by prodromal [[grafo/termos/protein aggregation|protein aggregation]] in the LC, late-stage degeneration of the LC, and comorbid conditions indicative of LC dysfunction. Many of these early studies were limited to post-mortem histological techniques due to the LC's small size and location deep in the [[grafo/termos/brainstem|brainstem]]. Thus, there is a growing interest in utilizing in vivo imaging of the LC as a predictor of preclinical neurodegenerative processes and biomarker of disease progression. Simultaneously, neuroimaging in animal models of neurodegenerative disease holds promise for identifying early alterations to LC circuits, but has thus far been underutilized. While still in its infancy, a handful of studies have reported effects of single gene mutations and pathology on LC function in disease using various neuroimaging techniques. Furthermore, combining imaging and optogenetics or [[grafo/termos/chemogenetics|chemogenetics]] allows for interrogation of network connectivity in response to changes in LC activity. The purpose of this article is twofold: (1) to review what [[grafo/termos/magnetic resonance imaging|magnetic resonance imaging]] (MRI) and positron emission tomography (PET) have revealed about LC dysfunction in neurodegenerative disease and its potential as a biomarker in humans, and (2) to explore how animal models can be used to test hypotheses derived from clinical data and establish a mechanistic framework to inform LC-focused [[grafo/termos/therapeutic interventions|therapeutic interventions]] to alleviate symptoms and impede disease progression.
