[[grafo/termos/dopamine|Dopamine]] is known to modulate [[grafo/termos/basal ganglia circuitry|basal ganglia circuitry]]. Previous studies have demonstrated that sustained [[grafo/termos/dopamine release|dopamine release]] in the [[grafo/termos/dorsal striatum|dorsal striatum]] is essential for regulating movement vigor. However, this has been recently challenged by da Silva and colleagues, who reported that [[grafo/termos/dopaminergic neurons|dopaminergic neuron]] activity generates transient dopamine release that triggers movement onset and, subsequently, movement vigor. Most patients with Parkinson’s disease experience slowness of movement and movement initiation difficulties, but the exact [[grafo/termos/mechanisms underlying|mechanisms underlying]] these movement difficulties have yet to be clarified. Recent studies have revealed that dopaminergic neurons tend to be transiently and rapidly activated before spontaneous movement onset. However, it is not yet clear whether transient activation of dopaminergic neurons contributes to movement initiation and ongoing movement. To address this problem, da Silva and colleagues implanted a 16-channel movable electrode bundle in the [[grafo/termos/substantia nigra pars compacta|substantia nigra pars compacta]] of mice that allowed them to use optogenetics to control the activity of dopaminergic neurons. First, the authors confirmed that dopaminergic neuron activity increased transiently before movement initiation. Next, they found that dopaminergic neuron activity promoted movement onset in mice during immobility; in contrast, movement initiation was impaired when dopaminergic neuron activity was turned off. However, when movement had already started, the inhibition of dopaminergic neurons did not alter movement vigor, the quickness of the ongoing movement was retained, and the movement could be completed. Thus, dopaminergic neuron activity was not required for maintaining an ongoing movement, which suggests that their transient activity is critical for the modulation of movement onset and vigor of future movements. This study revealed a previously unknown role of dopamine in regulating movement initiation and vigor. These results have been supported by a more recent study that investigated the mechanisms of dopamine release; dopamine secretion was rapid, transient, and had a high initial release rather than a sustained secretion. Combined, the results of both studies indicate that dopamine may act as a switch for opening or closing the door of movement. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution for Parkinson’s disease, as the studies were conducted with normal physiological dopaminergic neurons. It has yet to be seen whether similar results can be obtained in a model of Parkinson’s disease. Considering the importance of transient dopamine release in mediating movement initiation and ongoing movement, treatments that focus on transient activity of dopaminergic neurons might be an attractive [[grafo/termos/therapeutic target|therapeutic target]] for Parkinson’s disease. For example, an automatic and adjustable system that can be tailored to a patient’s individual condition, such as closed-loop [[grafo/termos/deep brain stimulation|deep brain stimulation]], may be beneficial for ameliorating the movement symptoms of Parkinson’s disease.
