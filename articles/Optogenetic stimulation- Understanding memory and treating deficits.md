Technology allowing [[grafo/termos/genetically targeted|genetically targeted]] cells to be modulated by light has revolutionized neuroscience in the past decade, and given rise to the field of optogenetic stimulation. For this, non-native, light activated proteins (e.g., [[grafo/termos/channelrhodopsin|channelrhodopsin]]) are expressed in a [[grafo/termos/specific cell|specific cell]] phenotype (e.g., [[grafo/termos/glutamatergic neurons|glutamatergic neurons]]) in a subset of [[grafo/termos/central nervous|central nervous]] system nuclei, and short pulses of light of a narrow wavelength (e.g., blue, 473 nm) are used to modulate [[grafo/termos/cell activity|cell activity]]. Cell activity can be increased or decreased depending on which light activated protein is used. We review how the greater precision provided by optogenetics has transformed the study of [[grafo/termos/neural circuits|neural circuits]], in terms of [[grafo/termos/cognition|cognition]] and behavior, with a focus on learning and [[grafo/termos/memory|memory]]. We also explain how optogenetic modulation is facilitating a better understanding of the mechanistic underpinnings of some neurological and psychiatric conditions. Based on this research, we suggest that optogenetics may provide tools to improve memory in [[grafo/termos/neurological conditions|neurological conditions]], particularly diencephalic amnesia and Alzheimer's disease.
